Mykhailo Pidburtnyi, Bryan Zanca, Claude Coppex, Santiago Jimenez-Villegas,

ABSTRACT: Mixed ionic and electronic conductors (MIECs) are being studied
extensively as a potential replacement for cermet catalysts in solid oxide cells (SOCs)?
collectively named for solid oxide fuel cells and solid oxide electrolysis cells?due to
their high activity, great stability, and low cost. Perovskite-type LaFeO3-based catalysts
are one of the most promising electrodes for SOCs. This review paper provides an
update on the chemical composition?crystal structure?physical property relationships
and an understanding of the electrochemical reaction mechanisms in the perovskite-type
structure LaFeO3 electrodes. Mechanisms for electrochemical CO2 reduction on the
perovskite structure surface and oxygen diffusion are discussed. Furthermore, the
electrochemical performance enhancement strategies such as composite electrodes and nanoparticles ex-solution are briefly
presented together with industrialization issues, e.g., gas impurities and stability of the interconnecting materials.
1. INTRODUCTION
High temperature solid oxide electrolysis cells (SOECs) are a
promising method for syngas production and organic
chemicals synthesis. The main advantage of the SOEC is
that fossil fuels burning byproducts can be used as a feeding
gas, which can reduce greenhouse gas emissions. The SOEC
can be used in the fuel cell mode to produce electricity with a
high efficiency (Figure 1). Many different materials are
currently being developed to be used as electrode catalysts
for SOECs. There are some requirements such as high catalytic
activity for redox reactions, stability in operating environments,
and chemical stability with cell components. The most obvious
candidate is a widely used composite Ni?Y-doped ZrO2 (Ni-
YSZ) cermet electrode for solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs).
However, in metal-ceramic electrodes the electrochemical
reactions only occur at the so-called triple-phase boundary
(TPB) where the ionic conducting phase (YSZ), electronic
conducting phase (Ni), and gas phase meet. Hence, the cell
performance is limited by the availablity of the TPB length.1
Another issue is that Ni particles oxidize when water and
water/CO2 coelectrolysis is taking place, which leads to NiO
formation and a loss of electronic conductivity.2 Moreover, Ni-
YSZ cermets are prone to carbon formation, sulfur poisoning,
and Ni segregation.3,4
Mixed ionic and electronic conductors (MIECs) are being
developed to replace the Ni-YSZ electrode. These MIEC
materials demonstrate high catalytic activity, redox stability,
and sulfur tolerance compared to Ni-YSZ.5,6 Furthermore,
several MIECs have demonstrated high stability in CO2/CO at

2. STRUCTURE PROPERTIES OF LaFeO3 AND ITS
RELATED MATERIALS
The naturally occurring perovskite mineral, CaTiO3, has
simple origins, but the complexity built around the synthetic
perovskite-like structure with a general chemical formula of
ABX3 (A = mono-, di-, and or trivalent cations; B = tri-, tetra-,
and pentavalent cations; X = mono-, di-, and trivalent anions).
The ideal cubic perovskite crystal structure is built up of AX3
close packed layers with 1/4 of the octahedral holes filled with
B-site cations. The A-site cation is usually large (?1.4 Å) and
coordinated by 12 anions, and the B-site is small (?0.6 Å) and
octahedrally coordinated by anions. The B-site octahedra form
a framework of corner sharing octahedra, and depending on
the size of the B-site cation different interstices spaces are
created that can accommodate different ionic radius A-site
cations in the perovskite framework.
The crystal structure of a primitive cubic perovskite ABO3 ?
SrTiO3 is shown in Figure 2. The ideal cubic perovskite
structure is not very common due to the lack of cations with
the ideal ionic radii on their ideal crystallographic sites. When
the A-site cation is smaller than the Sr2+ ion, a phenomenon
known as octahedral tilting occurs. This leads to structural
modifications that produce interesting electrical and magnetic
properties. As the perovskite structure diverts from the ideal
cubic form, octahedral tilts act to stabilize the structure, and
the degree or the nature of tilting has been explained using a
Glazer Notation.17 The distortions in perovskites can usually
be explained using three common principles which include
size, a change in oxidation states, and Jahn?Teller effects.18,19
In order to determine the stability of possible perovskite
materials, Goldschmidt developed a formula that uses the
geometric structural identity of the perovskite structure and
the ionic radii of the A- and B-site cations (rA and rB) and
anion (rO).

LaFeO3 Structural Characteristics. LaFeO3 has been
proposed as a promising cathode for the intermediate
temperature (IT) SOFCs and for the electrochemical
reduction of CO2 in SOECs.21,22 This material displays
MIEC properties that can be controlled both intrinsically
and extrinsically.23,24 Therefore, a good understanding of the
crystal structure is important to understand the nature of
defects that control the functional properties of the LaFeO3
material. The structure of undoped LaFeO3 adopts an
orthorhombic (space group Pbnm (No. 62)) perovskite
arrangement at ambient temperatures and up to ?957 °C.
The crystal structure of LaFeO3 is shown in Figure 3.21 The
unit cell at room temperature has four structural units (Z = 4)
and edge lengths of a = 5.55 Å, b = 5.56 Å, and c = 7.86 Å.21
Tables 1 and 2 list the structural parameters of LaFeO3. There
are two oxygen inequivalent atomic positions; the first oxygen
(O1) is tetrahedrally coordinated by two lanthanum cations
and two iron cations and sits within the layers of lanthanum,
and the second oxygen (O2) is coordinated by three
lanthanide cations and two iron cations and rests between
the layers of iron. The Goldschmidt tolerance factor for
LaFeO3 is 0.9543, which indicates that this material should
crystallize in the cubic perovskite structure. This deviation
from the cubic form is commonly caused by the A-site cation
being too small to fit in the interstices of the B-site cations.18
The structure is stabilized by neighboring octahedra tilting in
opposite directions; this can be described using glazer notation
as a+b?b? or a+a?a? depending on the space group setting
(Pbmn vs Pnma, respectively).18 The two tilting rotations are
opposite in direction (out-of-phase) but equal in magnitude.
The unit cell can be related to a pseudocubic perovskite lattice
by a ? b ? 21/2ap and c ? 2ap. The expansion of the unit cell is
related to the degree of octahedral tilting observed. The nature
of the octahedral tilt observed in LaFeO3 has been discussed
by Woodward to be caused by a strong covalent bond
interaction between lanthanum and oxygen, and the structure
distorts in order to maximize the degree of orbital overlap.19
This displacement of La3+ cations is projected along the ab-
plane.

from orthorhombic to rhombohedral between ?960 and

Role of A-Site Doping in LaFeO3 Phase Properties.
While LaFeO3 is inferior compared to its doped counterparts,
it remains critical to understand both the parent structure and
how it changes when doped with different cations. The alkaline
earth cations (Ca2+, Sr2+, and Ba2+) are the most common A-
site dopants. The ionic radius of calcium is very close to that of
lanthanum, while strontium and barium are much larger. The
most common dopant used to manipulate the ionic
conductivity of this material is Sr2+,7,34,35,37,38 which has a
larger ionic radius (?0.1 Å larger) and forms more ionic bonds
with oxygen. LaFeO3 has two compensation mechanisms that
can take place when the material is substituted with lower
valence cations, the iron can change its valence, oxygen
vacancies can be created, or some combination of both.
Therefore, the incorporation of these dopants is typically used
to control the ionic conductivity of the material. Strontium has
a larger solubility limit and increases the oxide ion conductivity
more than that of calcium and barium members.
When Sr is doped into the LaFeO3 structure
(La1?xSrxFeO3??), it appears to randomly disperse on the A-
site of the perovskite lattice, as no cation or oxygen vacancy
ordering is observed. The solubility of Sr in LaFeO3 is quite
unique as there are several phase transitions and two-phase
regions involved; this is shown in Figure 6. The orthorhombic
(Pnma) structure manifests between 0 ? x ? 0.2, after which a
two-phase region spans until x = 0.4 where both the
orthorhombic and rhombohedral phases exist.34 The addition
of Sr continuously shrinks the unit cell over the orthorhombic
region indicating, that Fe has oxidized or oxygen vacancies
have been induced. The rhombohedral phase has been shown
as phase pure between 0.40 ? x ? 0.6. A second mixed phase
region is observed between 0.7 < x < 0.8 where the
rhombohedral and cubic phases both exist. Finally, the
structure takes a cubic form between 0.8 ? x ? 1.0 in
La1?xSrxFeO3??.

Role of B-Site Doping in LaFeO3 Phase Properties.
The B-site of LaFeO3 is commonly doped with transition metal
cations due to similar ionic radii between the dopant and iron.
The effects of Cr, Mn, Co, Ni, and Cu have been extensively
studied; as well, the effects of Mg, Al, Sc, Ti, V, Ga, Nb, Mo,
Ru, Rh, Pd, In, Ta, and Ir have also been inves-
tigated.26,40?42,47,53,58,60?65 The most common dopants are
Co and Ni as these late transition metals can change the
structure from orthorhombic to rhombohedral. The solid
solution between LaFeO3 and LaNiO3 is smooth with a
composition phase transition around the 50?50 composition
from Pnma to R3?c.30,31 This composition commonly displays
both phases due to a first order phase transition near room
temperature; the structure is orthorhombic below ?27 °C and
rhombohedral above ?27 °C.31 The most studied composition
is LaFe0.8Co0.2O3?? due to its relationship to the codoped high-
performance phase La0.6Sr0.4Co0.2Fe0.8O3??.
When LaFeO3 is doped with Co, the structure remains
orthorhombic up to x = 0.40 in LaFe1?xCoxO3?? and
transitions from orthorhombic to rhombohedral at about the
50?50 composition. A small multiphase region spans between
0.40 < x < 0.60 where both the rhombohedral and
orthorhombic phases coexist.28,29 LaFeO3 has been codoped
with both Sr and Co with the following chemical formula,
La0.6Sr0.4CoyFe1?yO3?? (y = 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3), in order to
enhance both the ionic and the electronic conductivities.66?70
The most studied composition is La0.6Sr0.4Co0.2Fe0.8O3??,
which has a decent p-type electronic conductivity and high
catalytic activity for oxygen exchange.2?4 This material has also
been studied at temperatures as high as 1000 °C in SOECs for
CO2 reduction.
2 The thermal stability of this composition was
investigated by coulometric titrations as a function of partial
oxygen pressure to elucidate the redox state. The results
indicate that this composition is stable at 750 °C and 10?5
atm.3 The structure remains rhombohedral at 1000 °C, which
may be an indication that the rhombohedral structure has a

The effects of Cr and Mn should also be mentioned as they
are beneficial to dope multivalent transition metals into the
structure to assist with the catalytic function of CO2 reduction.
A full solid solution between LaFeO3 and LaCrO3 can be
achieved.71 These compositions crystallize in the orthorhombic
space group Pbnm. The main composition of interest is
LaFe0.5Cr0.5O3 as the conductivity reaches a maximum.72 This
composition also displays excellent oxygen evaluation reaction
(OER) performance.73 The addition of manganese, La-
Fe1?xMnxO3, drives a disorder induced localization of charge
carriers. This in turn increases the overall electrical
conductivity as a function of Mn content, up to x = 0.5.74
The catalytic performance of Mn-substituted LaFe1?xMnxO3
samples exhibits a low charge transfer resistance and abundant
oxygen ion species on the surface of the material to enhance its
catalytic performance.75
To summarize, the A-site is typically doped with alkaline
earth ions to control the ionic conductivity of the doped
LaFeO3 material. A necessary modification is to make sure the
reduction of CO2 is driven in an efficient manner, as the
diffusion of oxygen through the crystal structure is limited.
Doping Sr is typically preferred over Ca and Ba due to higher
overall conductivities, which is intensified by higher solubility
limits and disorder caused by cation ionic radii mismatch. The
B-site is responsible for the catalytic sequestration of CO2 and
electron transfer that initiates the reduction of CO2 into useful
chemical streams. The usage of multivalent cations such as Cr
or Mn on the B-site helps to improve both electronic
conductivity of the material and electron transfer between
the electrode material and the feedstock gas.
3. OXIDE ION TRANSPORT IN LaFeO3-BASED
PEROVSKITES
A high oxygen conductivity is very important for efficient
SOEC operation; thus, the enhancement of the oxide ion
conductivity and a better understanding of the transport
mechanisms within the crystal lattice are critical. This is
especially important for MIEC catalysts, which often have
much lower ionic conductivity, compared to solid electrolytes.
Recent studies have been mainly focused on improving the
transport properties, especially for perovskite-type structure
compounds.76 Oxygen migration in a solid is typically driven
by oxygen vacancies, which are present within the crystal
lattice. There are two important parameters that can explain
the oxide conductivity in oxides, which include the oxygen
vacancy concentration and the vacancy migration energy
barrier. Both are experimentally measurable and commonly
used in computational methods to predict the impact of
dopants on the material’s conductivity.77 For a more detailed
discussion regarding the success of computational modeling
involving molecular dynamics and DFT of perovskite-type
MIECs, the reader is referred to the papers published
elsewhere.78,79
DFT+U is often used to predict the formation energy of
oxygen vacancies. Lee et al. were able to calculate the
formation energy values and defect concentration for LaMnO3
in temperatures ranging between 600 and 1200 °C.80 The
obtained data was in good agreement with the experimental
values reported previously. In addition, valuable information
regarding the defect chemistry and oxygen tracer diffusivity
was obtained. Another study demonstrated that single crystals
of LaFeO3 and LaCoO3 had a higher tracer diffusion
coefficient (D0) for oxide ions when the concentration of the
oxygen vacancies was increased. The proportional relationship
between D0 and the oxygen partial pressure (pO2) was
established. These findings lead to the conclusion that the
oxygen diffusion in this perovskite structure follows a vacancy
migration mechanism. It was also shown that increasing the
substitution of La3+ with Sr2+ led to either more Fe4+/Co4+ or
more oxide ion vacancies, and an increase in the oxide ion
conductivity was observed. Sr-doping in LaFeO3 is a well-
established method for increasing the oxygen ion conductiv-
ity.81,82 In both LaFeO3 and LaCoO3, vacancy formation is
observed with a dominating redox process and a lower vacancy
formation energy.83?85

Considering there are two distinct crystallographic positions
for oxygen, each oxygen position has the potential to migrate
under a different mechanism in LaFeO3. The first oxygen
position (O1) lies on the same level as the lanthanum cations,
and oxygen in this position acts as anaxial oxygens in the iron
octahedra; the other oxygen position (O2) acts as the
equatorial oxygen positions in the layers of iron cations. The
vacancy formation energy only differs by 0.04 eV between two
oxygen positions.76 Thus, oxygen diffusion can follow three
different pathways (from O1 to O1, O2 to O2, or O1 to O2).
Calculations indicate that the diffusion from O1 to O2 gives
the lowest enthalpy. The calculated bond distances and lattice
parameters that allowed the orthorhombic structure to be
reproduced were found to be in a good agreement with
experimental values. In another study, DFT+U showed similar
pathways for the O1?O1 and O2?O2 and a different pathway
was obtained for the O1?O2.77
The process of calculating the migration barriers and
vacancy formation energies for all possible perovskite-type
structure compounds is very cumbersome (Figure 7)86 and
time-consuming. The high magnetization of Fe ions and
antiferromagnetic structure of LaFeO3 make it challenging for
DFT calculations. Recent studies include Hubbard U
corrections which utilize optimized U parameters against
experimentally measured lattice parameters and magnet-
ization.84,85,91 Comparing vacancy formation energies or
migration barriers in one compound using different computa-
tional methods can lead to great discrepancies, and limited
information is inferred. However, using the computational
methods, trends can be derived and could be related to the
experimental study.
The stability of lanthanum-based perovskites with different

Main Approaches for Electrochemical Performance
Enhancement. From a thermodynamic point of view, it is
more profitable to run SOECs at a high temperature due to a
lower electrical energy demand, decreased reaction over
potential, and faster oxygen ion diffusion.102,103 In practice,
high operation temperatures not only limit the range of
materials that can be used for cell production but also increase
the costs of cell manufacturing and operation. High temper-
ature operation dramatically enhances the material degradation
rate.104 The obvious way to increase the long-term stability and
decrease cell manufacturing costs is to reduce the cell
operation temperature. The usage of a composite electrode
helps not only to overcome the high activation energy issue but
also to reduce incompatibility between components due to the
mismatch between thermal expansion coefficients as well as
decrease the particle coarsening rate. This approach also helps
to combine different materials to eliminate their disadvantages
and thus synergistically increase preferred operational charac-
teristics. There are two different ways to make the composite
electrodes that are widely used by researchers in SOCs.
Mechanical Mixing. The first approach is mechanical
mixing of the individual components, predominantly the
catalyst and the electrolyte, followed by surface deposition of
the obtained slurry. Xi et al. have shown that the highly active
composite La0.6Sr0.4Co0.2Fe0.8O3?? (LSCF)?Ce0.9Gd0.1O2??
(GDC) electrode can be obtained by mechanical processing
of La2O3, Sr(OH)2, Co3O4, Fe2O3, and GDC powders within a
short period of time (around 20 min) and then sintering at 900
°C for 2 h.105 The symmetrical half-cell tests utilizing a dense
GDC layer as an electrolyte in air have shown very low
polarization resistances of 0.08, 0.17, 0.38, and 0.69 Ohm·cm2
at temperatures of 800, 750, 700, and 650 °C correspondingly.
Those values are close to the previously reported high-
performance composite cathodes, obtained by the more
expensive and time-consuming precursor infiltration meth-
od.106

Durability during Electrochemical Operation and
Degradation Mechanisms. In order to increase the ionic
conductivity of LaFeO3 derivatives, the number of oxygen
vacancies must be increased. The most common approach is to
partially substitute cations on the A-site. Lanthanum (3+) is
replaced by an ion with a similar size and lower oxidation state,
such as strontium (2+). It has been shown by many researchers
that strontium tends to segregate on the surface of the
perovskite that is believed to be the main reason for LSCF
degradation.123?126 In order to better understand the potential
role of Sr segregation in the LSCF catalyst degradation
mechanism, Oh et al.127 investigated the elemental enrichment
behavior of this material surface. After the sample went
through a heat treatment in air for 50?100 h in the
temperature range of 600?900 °C, submicrometer-sized
particles had formed on the surface of the grains. Further
investigation by Auger electron spectroscopy revealed that the
precipitate was composed of strontium and oxygen, and EDX
did not show any significant changes in the bulk composition.
The amount of precipitate formed increased with an increasing
temperature and oxygen partial pressure. A two-step
mechanism for the formation of Sr-rich particles was proposed.
First, oxygen vacancies are formed, and then precipitation of a
Sr-enriched phase occurs. The rate of the first step increases
with a decrease in oxygen partial pressure. The second step
occurs faster in oxidizing conditions. It is well-known that
more precipitants are formed when the oxygen partial pressure
increases, thus suggesting that the second step seems to be rate
determining.

Another factor that can dramatically decrease the activity of
Sr-containing perovskite catalysts is Cr poisoning.129?131 The
most common source of Cr in the cells is the metallic alloys
that are used as cell interconnects. Cr might also be introduced
by some of the supporting equipment, such as heat exchangers,
tubing, and so on. Usually poisoning is caused by the reduction
of Cr(VI) species to solid chromium particles, which can
deposit on the catalyst surface.132 It has been proposed that
this reduction is due to a kinetically controlled chemical
reaction between gaseous Cr(VI) species and the Sr-rich
segregated phases. The most common impurity phases that
have been observed include SrCrO4 and Cr2O3. Large (up to 6
?m) SrCrO4 deposits can form and block pores in the catalyst.
This is a problem because this phase has a low electrical
conductivity and low catalytic activity.
The alternative degradation mechanism has been predicted
by Ni et al.133 who observed that higher degradation was
achieved for surfaces with homogeneous Cr distribution and
low deposited Cr. This is in the contrast to the electrodes
which have low Cr contents in the bulk and large amounts of
chromium surface deposits.134 The degradation due to Cr
poisoning of the (La0.6Sr0.4)0.95(Co0.2Fe0.8)O3?? (LSCF6428)
cathode was investigated in a symmetrical cell mode. EIS data
analysis (Figures 18 and 19) have clearly shown that with
overall Cr content about 2.7 atom % k0 and D0 dropped by 1?
2 orders of magnitude. Even at a high poisoning level, focused
ion beam scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM) tomog-
raphy did not show any significant changes at the micrometer
scale. On the contrary, the incorporation of chromium was
clearly seen at the nanoscale level (Figure 20). In addition, the
Fe?Co spinel formation was readily increased by Cr poisoning,
which might be a consequence of partial B-site cation
substitution with chromium. Also, initial evidence of Cr
segregation along LSCF grain boundaries was presented, which
is associated with a Co deficiency. The methods developed in
the presented study provided several new ways to examine the
degradation mechanism in SOEC/SOFCs that can be used for
testing the stability of new materials operating under real
conditions.

boundary length reduction; SrZrO3 formation; and a ceria-
zirconia solid solution formation.
5. ROLE OF FEEDING GAS IMPURITIES ON THE
PERFORMANCE OF LaFeO3-BASED SOECs
The formation of syngas by high temperature electrolysis needs
a mixture of H2O and CO2 supplied to the cathodes surface.
139
As SOECs are a sustainable technology, a variety of different
potential fuels have been considered. Each fuel has the
potential to introduce different impurities that will vary
depending on the fuel’s initial composition, for instance, sulfur
impurities, which are common for fossil fuels and biogas which
could produce sulfur dioxide or hydrogen sulfide during the
operation. H2S is being widely reported as an activity inhibitor
for Ni-YSZ cermet anodes in SOFC due to Ni surface
poisoning.140,141 Several MIECs appeared to be sulfur
tolerant142?144 and were exhaustively discussed by Gong et
al.;145 in some cases, they also enhance the electrochemical
activity.146
Addo et al. tested the performance of La0.3M0.7Fe0.7Cr0.3O3??
(M = Sr, Ca) as the full cell anodes in the presence of 9 ppm of
H2S at 800 °C.147 In the presence of H2S, the polarization
resistance dropped and was 4% smaller rather than in the initial
gas composition (wet H2:N2 = 30:70). It was suggested that
some adsorbed species like FeS might have formed, although
no additional experiments were conducted to support this
argument. Wenyi et al. have shown that perovskite structured
material can be inactivated by the presence of H2S.

In any case, strontium sulfate formation leads to a performance

Another class of common impurities include the nitrogen
oxides (NOx) that usually form when fossil fuels are burned at
elevated temperature. As NOx severely contributes to the
formation of smog and acid rain, some efforts were attempted
to convert them to nitrogen in the SOC with a perovskite
catalyst, such as LSM.59,151?153 None of these studies found
anything to report regarding perovskite catalyst degradation.
Another group of possible impurities includes the halogen-
containing compounds. Predominantly, flue gas contains
hydrogen chloride and hydrogen fluoride. To our best of
knowledge, a few groups have tested the influence of flue gases
with MIEC catalysts in SOCs. Li et al. has shown during a 1 h
test that no degradation was achieved in a hydrogen fuel cell
with a HCl concentration of 8 ppm at 850 °C and 10 ppm at
750 °C.154 Trembly et al. reported a 17.4% loss of anode
performance at 800 °C when a 20 ppm of HCl concentration
was utilized.157 The reaction between Ni and HCl to form
NiCl2 was suggested.155 Hence, even low concentrations of
HCl can significantly reduce the performance of the MIEC
electrodes when metal nanoparticles are present on the surface.
In addition, hydrogen halides are highly corrosive, especially at
high temperatures. This may cause degradation of the metallic
components of the supporting equipment and current
collectors as well as ceramic materials that are used for
sealing.156,157
Aromatic hydrocarbons have been found to easily form coke
on the catalyst active sites, which causes a drastic drop in the
activity. This is likely caused by a slow reaction rate due to the
presence of strong C?C bonds.158 This was proven by many
researchers studying cermets, such as Ni-YSZ or Cu-
CeO2.
159?161 Nevertheless, this coke formation is predom-
inantly reversible and can be removed after pure fuel is
supplied.159,161 Lee et al. reported that during the diesel
reforming reactions on the perovskite catalyst with composi-
tions La0.8Sr0.2Cr0.95Ru0.05O3 and Sm0.8Ba0.2Cr0.95Ru0.05O3 coke
formation can be observed.158 It was proposed that the
catalytic reaction is inhibited due to the occupation of active
sites by carbon deposits. As coke formation is observed for
both perovskite structured materials without cell polarization
and a cermet catalyst, the same behavior could be expected for
LaFeO3 derived materials. There are many other compounds
that can potentially be present in trace amounts such as AsH3,
H2Se, PH3, Sb, Hg, Cd, and Pb in the SOC feedstocks.162 Even
though the quantity of these impurities is much smaller, their
influence on the SOC performance is currently unknown and
needs to be deeply investigated.
6. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, LaFeO3-based materials have the potential to
replace cermet materials as electrodes in SOCs due to their
high catalytic activity. Here, we reviewed the following key
points about this material and its application in SOECs.
• Lanthanum ferrite-based materials crystallize in three
distinct crystal structures based on temperature and
composition, the orthorhombic phase is dominant at low
temperatures and in low A-site doping systems, the
rhombohedral phase evolves at higher temperatures or
with heavily doped systems, and a cubic phase is usually
only seen at high temperatures. The rhombohedral
phase seems to be the preferred crystal structure due to
LaFeO3 catalytic activity at ?800 °C.
• The oxygen diffusion pathways in LaFeO3 are important
for understanding how the structure changes at the local
scale when the material is operating as a catalyst at high
temperatures. Several possible pathways have been
computationally proposed using DFT studies.
• The catalytic performance of these materials could be
improved by making composites or nanoparticle ex-
solution techniques. Both techniques have shown
promise to improve the catalytic performance along
with lowering the cost and making more durable cells.
• Lanthanum ferrite-based materials have some disadvan-
tages such as poisoning by impurities in the feed gas.
Several studies have shown that properties of LaFeO3
