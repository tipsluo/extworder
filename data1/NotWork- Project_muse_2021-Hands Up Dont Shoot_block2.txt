shoot / guns. Poems that wrestle cops into alleys / and take their weapons leaving 
them dead.”2 Baraka believed the Black aesthetic could galvanize the Black com-

Black women activists were at the forefront of this cultural revolution that was tak-
ing place on theatrical stages, reimagining the centrality of Black women’s subjectiv-
ity—their experiential knowledge, concerns, and desires—within the framework of 
the Black liberation struggle. For example, activists Sonia Sanchez, Lola E. Jones, and 
Carol Freeman stood among a cadre of artists-activists who used their creative talents 
to advocate for Black women without in any way negating concern for the collective 
well-being of Black families.4 Sanchez, Jones, and Freeman formulated new images of 
Black womanhood to demonstrate the complexities of Black women, their unique ex-
periences of injustice, and the need for their ongoing leadership within Black political 
mobilization efforts.5 However, in celebrating Black women’s deep commitment to the 
survival of Black families, Sanchez, Jones, and Freeman also issue a call for spectators 
to realize a more inclusive vision of Black liberation. Unfortunately, biases within the 
academy have amplified masculine-centered visions of Black liberation while simul-
taneously contributing to the erasure of Black women theatre artists’ more nuanced 
approach to the Black liberation struggle.

While celebrated and, at times, criticized by their peers, Sanchez’s, Jones’s, and 
Freeman’s woman-centered works faded into obscurity following the decline of the 
Black Arts Movement. Sanchez, perhaps the most recognizable name among these 
three Black women artists today, commanded respect from her comrades, but also ran 
afoul of some Black nationalists men who challenged her woman-centered writings. 
Sanchez recalls one instance while working in the attic of Baraka’s Black Arts Repertory 
Theatre/School (BART/S, 1965) when a male “comrade” held a gun to her head and 
demanded that she cease writing.6 Despite this threat to her life, Sanchez continued to 
publish works that center Black women. Jones, a short-story writer, playwright, and 
English professor, did not achieve the national reputation of either Sanchez or Free-
man. However, her woman-centered dramas energized the Black revolution on college 
campuses across the country.7 Freeman, a Louisiana native and Oakland resident, rose 
to national prominence as an award-winning poet of the Black Arts Movement. The 
Suicide, her only published play, represents the sole Black woman dramatist’s voice 
in Baraka and Larry Neal’s seminal collection, Black Fire (1968). As a self-described 
“revolutionary Black nationalist,” Freeman’s poetry and drama invoke lynching and 
Black women’s mourning to provoke collective action.8

the academy has led, and continues to lead, to the marginalization of Black women 
artists within theatre history scholarship. While their works have been less studied or 
entirely ignored by theatre historians, the revolutionary dramas of Sanchez, Freeman, 
and Jones made profound contributions to Black Power and Black feminist discourses. 
In this essay, I examine the discourses in Sanchez’s The Bronx Is Next (1968), Jones’s 
The New Nigger, or Who’s Afraid of William Faulkner (1976), and Freeman’s The Suicide 
(1966) in order to illuminate that collectively, their works demonstrate that “none of 
us are free until all of us are free,” a mantra that women leaders of the Black Lives 
Matter movement (2013–present) have now embraced.9

type: BODY

The second part of this essay explores the symbolic relationship among race, gender, 
and gun violence in four revolutionary dramas of the Black Arts Movement. I begin by 
exploring Baraka’s Arm Yourself, Or Harm Yourself (1967), a play that engages with the 
Black Power rhetoric of gun ownership, hegemonic masculinity, and white-authored 
gun violence. I then consider Sanchez’s, Jones’s, and Freeman’s engagements with 
Baraka’s representative work. I situate their (at times) controversial dramas within 
Black women’s long-standing activist tradition, examining how their works produce 
womanist thought that complicates Black Power rhetoric of self-defense and masculinist 
understandings of Black liberation. By “womanist,” I refer to the Black feminist term 
coined by Alice Walker in her book, In Search of Our Mother’s Gardens: Womanist Prose 
(1983). Walker writes that a womanist “loves other women, sexually and/or nonsexu-
ally. Appreciates and prefers women’s culture, women’s emotional flexibility (values 
tears as natural counterbalance of laughter), and women’s strength. Sometimes loves 
individual men, sexually and/or nonsexually. Committed to the survival and whole-
ness of entire people, male and female.”10 While the works of women dramatists of 
the Black Arts Movement pre-date Walker’s Black feminist theorization; their dramas 
nonetheless provide an early expression of womanist thought. In detailing how the 
Black Arts Movement was less uniformly masculinist than it has sometimes been 
depicted, I also demonstrate how Sanchez’s, Jones’s, and Freeman’s early womanist 
interventions laid a critical foundation for the principles outlined by Black women 
activists who lead today’s Black Lives Matter movement.

The impetus for Black Arts Movement theatre emerged from mass demonstrations 
against white-authored shootings of Black people. Indeed, the 1960s witnessed hundreds 
of race-related uprisings—often incited by white police officers shooting unarmed Black 
men and boys—throughout major US cities.12 For example, the Harlem uprisings of 
1964 were sparked by the death of a Black 15-year-old named James Powell, a Bronx 
resident who was shot and killed by a white police officer in front of witnesses outside 
of an apartment building. Hundreds of Powell’s classmates gathered to protest his fatal 
shooting, followed by six consecutive nights of demonstrations by thousands of Har-
lem residents. As extrajudicial shootings persisted throughout the country, rebellions 
continued. The summer of 1967, known as the “Long, Hot Summer of 1967,” proved 
particularly volatile, resulting in 159 race-related uprisings across the United States. 
These rebellions stirred a generation of theatre artists, including Baraka, to create Black 
art that would sustain the hearts, minds, and souls of Black Americans.

Baraka envisioned revolutionary dramas as a theatre of assault, as weapons Black 
artists could wield to expose harsh truths, including the need for Black men to defend 
themselves and their communities from white male violence. Baraka incorporated guns 
into his street plays in order to draw spectators, making a spectacle of the symbolic 
meaning of Black gun ownership. He recalls one occasion when he sent a BART/S 
troupe member running down the street “with a pistol chasing one of the characters 
in Black Ice. [S]eeing a brother with a gun chasing somebody who looked like a white 
man made a crowd instantly, and the show began! ”15 Baraka’s formulations were in 
keeping with masculinist Black Power imagery of the time, as exemplified by the highly 
circulated 1967 image of Black Panther Party leader Huey P. Newton seated in a wicker 
chair. In the image, Newton stares defiantly at the camera, appearing as a prince on a 
throne framed by African cultural artifacts. Eschewing a sceptre, Newton holds a shot 
gun in his right hand and African hunting staff in his left. The image evokes a call to 
use weapons—Black gun ownership and culture—to restore Black men to their right-
ful place as kings and princes. Indeed, many Black activists—including members of 
the Black Panther Party—encouraged Black male gun ownership as an essential step 
toward reclaiming their “lost” manhood or kingdom. Baraka’s formulations tap into 
Black Panther Party imagery and rhetorical strategies, conceptualizing revolutionary 
theatre as a viable platform to help restore Black manhood (fig. 1).

The restoration of Black manhood—which some Black Panther Party activists per-
ceived as tantamount to the liberation of Black America—entailed the ability to defend 
and protect the Black community from white, male-authored violence. Increasing Black 
male gun ownership became the proposed method of defense and protection, and for 
some, liberation. The Black Panther Party reinforced this ideology through spectacu-
lar displays of Black-male gun ownership during public protests. Many white male 
politicians, including California’s then-governor Ronald Reagan, attempted to disarm 
the Black Panther Party. For example, one of their most noted protests featured thirty 
party members marching to the steps of the California State Capitol building armed 
with .45 caliber pistols, .357 Magnums, and 12-gauge shotguns. Their 1967 display 
led state politicians to pass the Mulford Act, effectively preventing Panthers from 
openly carrying loaded firearms during protests and “copwatching” patrols.16 While 
politicians may have curtailed their efforts, the Black Panther Party’s performances of 
Black power inspired Black activists and offered much-needed contrast from the all-
too-familiar sight of white men leveling guns at stripped, handcuffed, and motionless 
Black people (fig. 2).

Unfortunately, Baraka’s perpetuation of hegemonic masculinity excludes Black women 
from the paradigm of capable leadership within the Black liberation struggle. By “hege-
monic masculinity,” I refer to the hierarchal power structure that uses identities—such 
as gender, class, sexuality, age, and race—in opposition to one another to empower 
those closest to dominant social norms while marginalizing those furthest away.19 The 
majority of Black men lack access to wealthy white men’s political, economic, and so-
cial standing. Unable to claim traditional markers of white-defined masculine power, 
Black men’s “use of their bodies, physicality, and a form of masculine aggressiveness 
becomes more important.”20 Physical aggressiveness—dominating Black women, boys, 
gay men, and poor and working-class men of other races—provided a means of com-
manding “respect,” an essential aspect of Black manhood. In Arm Yourself, Second 
Brother’s acquisition of manhood is dependent upon Sister’s subordination. As such, 
Sister does not wield a gun, does not engage in the physical altercation between First 
Brother and Second Brother, and remains voiceless during their Black nationalist debate. 
When she does speak, she only reinforces her position as a passive victim of white, 
male-authored violence. For example, Sister explains that Paul’s initial injury occurred 
while exercising his male authority. “The policeman put his hands . . . on me . . . asked 
me for identification. . . . Paul hit him . . . and he shot Paul,” recounts Sister.21 First 
Brother responds to Sister’s experience of assault with apathy, which prompts Second 
Brother to warn him, “until you, we, get enough heart to walk with this power . . . to be 
men, brother, then we deserve just what we get.”22 Second Brother considers the white 
officer’s assault of Sister as an act of “disrespect” toward Black men. Therefore taking 
up the gun (“this power”) to protect Black women becomes an important expression 
of Black manhood. Sister, who is furthest removed from hegemonic masculinity, can 
only observe the Black liberation struggle, not actively participate.

Sanchez’s revolutionary drama The Bronx Is Next anticipates and actively sets the 
groundwork for contemporary Black (women) activists and organizers. The Bronx Is 
Next presents a group of young revolutionaries—Charles, Roland, Larry, and Jimmy—on 
the streets of Harlem, initiating a reverse migration South. As they gather residents to 
burn down their tenement houses, they converse with an elderly woman who refuses to 
abandon her belongings, a young mother named Black Bitch, and an empathetic White 
Cop who solicits sex from Black Bitch. Through their interactions, Sanchez refutes the 
criminalization of Black men, condemns white-authored shootings of Black men, and 
promotes Black gun ownership. However, Sanchez adds complexity to Black political 
thought through her scathing critique of performative white allyship and hegemonic 
masculinity within the Black liberation struggle. Yet The Bronx Is Next ultimately argues 
for a more radical vision of Black liberation founded upon strong Black families, with 
Black men and women working together in love, unity, and mutual respect.

The Bronx Is Next reflects the political concerns and debates that were happening 
among Black communities in Harlem. Upon the closure of BART/S in 1965, Sanchez 
left Harlem and moved to the San Francisco Bay area to teach alongside Baraka, Sarah 
Webster Fabio, Ed Bullins, and Marvin X at San Francisco State College. Along with 
helping organizers and student activists establish the first Black studies program in 
the United States, Sanchez published her first play, The Bronx Is Next, which premiered 
at Theatre Black (New York) in 1970.26 The Bronx Is Next dramatizes the Republic of 
New Afrika’s (1968–present) vision of establishing an independent Black nation in five 
US Southern states: Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, and South Carolina. 
According to Sanchez, the Nation of Islam had already purchased land in the South, 
and revolutionary leaders had begun endorsing burning down tenement houses as a

The  New  Nigger  serves  as  a  counterargument  to  Nobel  Prize–winner  William 
Faulkner’s infamous commentary about racial integration and white-male gun culture. 
During his 1956 interview with journalist Russell Warren Howe, Faulkner remarked: 
“The South is armed for revolt. After the Supreme Court decision [of May 17, 1954, on 
school integration] you couldn’t get as much as a few rounds for a deer rifle in Mis-
sissippi. The gunsmiths were sold out. These white people will accept another Civil 
War knowing they’re going to lose.”39 Later, Faulkner admits that although he would 
prefer navigating the “middle of the road,” he would “fight for Mississippi against 
the United States even if it meant going out into the street and shooting Negroes.”40 
Faulkner uses “South” and “Mississippi” as dog-whistle terms for the Southern white 
men who fear and resist integration as ardently as their ancestors feared and resisted 
emancipation. Responding to this perceived Black threat, everyday white men took 
up arms, endowing themselves with the power to kill Black people. Faulkner suggests 
that the answer to white terrorism lies in giving white men “time” to come to terms 
with integration: “Give him time—don’t force us. . . . Things have been getting bet-
ter slowly for a long time. Only six Negroes were killed by whites in Mississippi last 
year, according to the police figures.”41 The absurdity of phrases such as “things are 
getting better,” “only six Negroes were killed by whites,” and “police records” were 
not lost on Black intellectuals of the time. James Baldwin famously counterargued 
that one Black death at the hands of whites was too many, questioned the accuracy 
of police accounts, and insisted that many Southern Black men were not “afraid of 
changing the status quo.”42 The New Nigger supports Baldwin’s counterargument, 
demonstrating that Southern Blacks are not “afraid” of the world’s Faulkners or white 
male vigilantes. The New Nigger depicts Southern Black men ready to take up arms in 
pursuit of racial integration.

Freeman’s The Suicide also privileges Black women’s subjectivity without in any 
way negating concern for the collective well-being of all of Black America. The Suicide 
allows audiences to witness a Black woman’s devastation brought on by the extra-
legal murder of her intellectual husband. Freeman draws from Black women’s anti-
lynching playwriting tradition, centering the struggle for citizenship through Wife’s 
experiences. Koritha Mitchell writes, “because lynching plays reflect and encourage 
community conversation, the emergence of the Black mother/wife as a central figure 
registers the degree to which her experiences informed discussions of Black identity 
and citizenship.”52 Jones draws from this genre, using the typical one-act structure, the 
image of a devastated Black widow, the terror of a white-authored home invasion, and 
denial of Black citizenship to reveal the injustice of US laws. However, in centering the 
pain of Black widowhood, Freeman also considers the failures of Black clergymen and 
the need to redefine Black liberation as the survival of the Black family.

The Black Church has functioned as a powerful force for organizing Black resistance 
to white-authored violence, but it has also perpetuated white, middle-class ideologies 
that harm vulnerable members within the Black community, including women, non-
binary, and LGBTQ+ folk. Historically, Black clergymen have concerned themselves 
with the spiritual needs of their congregations. Many more have also led the masses 
in protest against racial oppression, functioning as spiritual advisors, civic activists, 
and political leaders within their communities. Black clergymen and their congrega-
tions have cultivated a politics of respectability—a form of racial uplift ideology that 
includes a concern for public appearance and the adoption of Victorian mores—in the 
hope of gaining white acceptance. “From its position of authority, the Black Church 
has shown strong support for the patriarchal family, claiming that men should be the 
heads of the church, that women should not be preachers, and that men should rule 
their families,” writes Collins.56 Jones’s apolitical Preacher embodies this popular con-
servative politics, caring more for the appearance of respectability than for galvanizing 
the Black community to agitate for social justice.

women and seek justice for all forms of white-authored violence. In  The Suicide, 
Preacher’s longing to view Frank’s corpse supersedes his sacred responsibility to mourn 
with Wife. Audiences learn that Preacher has never visited the Jones’s home and is, in 
fact, a stranger to them. Having read a report of Frank’s “suicide” in the newspaper, 
Preacher insists he has arrived to offer a prayer. Once seated, Preacher remains silent 
until Wife offers to let him view Frank’s body. Preacher responds, “Don’t mind if I 
do,” as casually as if he were accepting refreshments.57 Upon viewing Frank’s lynched 
body, Preacher stares in horror. Had he moved beyond voyeurism and fulfilled his 
Christian duty, Preacher might have helped her put together the suspicious circum-
stances of Frank’s supposed “suicide.” However, as a voyeur of Black trauma porn, 
Preacher says nothing to alleviate Wife’s suffering and does nothing to incite justice.

==============================
type: abstract
typeindex=0 left=1 right=363 upper=125 lower=204

charfont name=Palatino-Roman charfont height=6.600000, charfont bold=0, alignment=2, allupper=0, column left=1, likeBody=100

The experiences of artists and activists of the Black Arts and Black Power era continue 
to offer valuable insights into the contemporary Black liberation struggle. The dramas 
of Sanchez, Jones, and Freeman reinforce the reality that Black liberation involves 
collective effort. This profound truth is evident in Baraka’s recollection of the 1967 
Newark Rebellion, in which he took part. Baraka recalls his personal experience of 
nearly being murdered by the police—if not for the help of the Black community—in 
his unpublished essay, “Spirit House, The New Fortress” (2013). Baraka writes:
==============================

==============================

typeindex=-8 left=21 right=363 upper=218 lower=284
====>
charfont name=Palatino-Roman charfont height=5.900000, charfont bold=0, alignment=1, allupper=0, column left=1, likeBody=-100

We stayed out too late, even stopping to pick up a brother shot in the leg by the police and 
took him to Martland Hospital. Later he was a key witness in my trial. Since my friends 
and I were arrested later that evening as we had turned the car around to head home. 
We saw the red lights flashing, and we were stopped, pulled from the VW van and I was 
beaten, head split open, teeth loosened. I believe the police would have killed me except 
for the people in an apartment building who were witnessing the beating started hollering 
and throwing things.68
==============================

==============================
type: BODY
typeindex=0 left=1 right=363 upper=297 lower=388
====>
charfont name=Palatino-Roman charfont height=6.600000, charfont bold=0, alignment=2, allupper=0, column left=1, likeBody=100

From Baraka’s perspective, police officers used excessive force and failed to detect 
actual crime. Instead, they became judges, jury, and executioners, inflicting violence on 
those they pledged to serve and protect. While experiences such as this demonstrate 
the longstanding need to reform or defund the police, his story also offers a beacon of 
hope. In Baraka’s most desperate circumstances—when he thought he would surely 
die—it was the Black community of Newark that protected him. They did so by bear-
ing witness to the crime and actively participating in his struggle, demonstrating how 
crucial collective action is to our survival as a people (fig. 4).
==============================

==============================
type: BODY
typeindex=0 left=1 right=364 upper=399 lower=538
====>
charfont name=Palatino-Roman charfont height=6.600000, charfont bold=0, alignment=2, allupper=0, column left=1, likeBody=100

Collective action starts with recognizing that none of us are free until all of us are 
free. For this reason, I urge Black artists and activists to answer Charlene Carruthers’s 
call to embrace Black queer feminist praxis as a tool of empowerment. Founded upon 
the theories and practices of Black feminist and LGBTQ+ activists, Black queer feminist 
praxis fosters the liberation of all Black people, regardless of gender, sexuality, age, 
class, and so forth by allowing “people and groups to bring their full selves into the 
process of dismantling all systems of oppression.”69 Carruthers writes that by using this 
lens, “we can more effectively prioritize problems and methods that center historically 
marginalized people in our communities.”70 As the revolutionary theatre of the Black 
Arts Movement can attest, the theatre has served as a powerful tool to disseminate 
such calls. We must do more to support the efforts of Black artists and activists who 
answer this call today.

The erosion of civil rights protections, coupled with the ever-present threat of white 
male-authored shootings, attests to the urgent need to foster Black art and activism at 
every level of society. To put this in plain terms: the energy used to fuel the (mostly) 
white mob that stormed the US Capitol building on January 6, 2020, is the same energy 
that hung a noose around Black women and men a century ago. The energy that fuels 
anti-Black violence has never gone away, because in all situations—to hearken to the 
sentiments of Patricia Young—the perpetrators saw themselves as “agents to their white 
peers.”72 They were confident that as executioners of “justice,” they would avoid any 
accountability for their actions. We have lost generations of Black artists and activists 
to the everyday, less-sensationalized occurrence of white-authored shootings. Their 
premature deaths have had a profound impact on the lives of many, myself included.
==============================

==============================
type: BODY
typeindex=0 left=1 right=363 upper=161 lower=264
====>
charfont name=Palatino-Roman charfont height=6.600000, charfont bold=0, alignment=2, allupper=0, column left=1, likeBody=100

Having survived abject poverty and gun violence in one of the wealthiest countries 
in the world, I now ask: How can Black activists and their allies use theatre as a tool to 
further Black liberation? The Black Arts Movement empowered everyday Black people 
to take action and promote a cultural revolution that would redefine what it means 
to be human. I hope that twenty-first-century Black activists and their allies will learn 
from these efforts and create art that engages the masses and fosters the inclusive aims 
of the Black Lives Matter movement. I stand as a witness that if we continue to lose 
the gains made from their activism, we will see another generation of Black youth lost 
to incarceration and death.
