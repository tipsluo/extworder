What can geography as a discipline contribute towards a better understand-
ing of human rights issues? Based on an analysis of three maps, selected from 
a larger set of thirty-two generated from data abstracted from 720 Amnesty 
International Freedom Writers Letters, this study finds that the number and 
frequency of human rights abuses over the past twenty years has remained 
stable at best. A set of ten confounding factors are indentified to explain 
this lack of progress towards improving worldwide human rights. This article 
concludes that geographers and human rights scholars could profit in their 
work by collaborating and consulting with one another’s research.

endeavor examining the human condition, human rights must be part of its

At present, Honey notes that there are two traditions in the geographic 
approach to human rights. An older, implicit approach has focused on hu-
man rights problems such as racism, slavery, social justice, poverty, and 
electoral violations without specific reference to any extant human rights 
doctrines or agreements. In their presidential addresses, for example, many 
past presidents of the Association of American Geographers, such as Richard 
Hartshorne, Richard Morrill, Harold Rose, Gilbert White, and Julian Wolpert, 
have called for the eradication of these wrongs. In contrast, the second, more 
contemporary tradition has actively and formally advocated for democracy 
and pushed for civil and political liberties based on both the French and 
United States Revolutions. In some cases, scholars have lobbied or advocated 
for a communal rights agenda emphasizing social and economic benefits: 
wages, jobs, education, and health care.

The question I wish to explore is whether or not there has been a change 
in the prevalence of the spatial patterns of human rights abuses over the 
period 1986–2006. There are ample reasons to speculate that such changes 
might or should have occurred. The end of the Cold War, the fall of the So-
viet Union, the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis, the spread of globalization and 
economic development, and the end of the “dirty wars” in Latin America 
have all had the potential for enhancing respect for human rights and re-
ducing human rights abuses. So my working hypothesis will be that related 
sets of economic and political changes have in fact led to improvements in 
human rights, and that such improvements can be seen in an examination 
of maps showing human rights abuses over time. Possible changes in the 
spatial distribution of human rights abuses are dealt with as follows: first, 
the data set used will be presented; then, maps of the number, percent, and 
prevalence of human rights abuses will be described and analyzed. Finally, 
materials that can provide an explanation for the maps and an answer to 
the question regarding possible changes in the spatial distribution of rights 
abuses will be presented.

and cross-cultural analysis due to problems regarding the accuracy, validity, 
and comparability of data, as well as the differences in scale, methodolo-

twenty year history. This database is made up of the collected model letters

officials, each letter describing a specific case of human rights abuse in their 
country. In all, there were 720 letters available for use. The letters for the

first impression of the map. There is a wide range of what percent of each 
region’s countries were cited. The Pacific Basin (including Australia and 
New Zealand) and the Caribbean Basin appear relatively immune to human 
rights abuse. At the opposite end of the spectrum, the countries that make 
up the constellation of the Former Soviet Union—Armenia, Belarus, Georgia, 
Moldova, Russia, and Ukraine—seem particularly prone to abuses. Europe 
occupies a place slightly above the low end, while the remaining regions 
cluster towards the high end. A regional subdivision of Europe suggests that 
Eastern and Southern Europe are more prone to human rights abuses than 
the Western and Northern sections. A breakdown of Asia shows that only 
slightly more than a third of the countries in East Asia were cited, while the 
other regions are all in the high 80 percent to 90 percent range of abuse. 
Finally, by looking at the citation records for individual countries (Tables 3 
and 4) it is possible to see which countries are the chronic abusers of human 
rights. For each study period there appear to be on average fifteen chronic 
abusers. The lists do contain repeat offenders, such as China, Myanmar, and 
Syria, although there is also some movement of countries on and off these 
lists. However, China, Myanmar, and Syria have the dubious honor of having 
been cited in all four time periods of the study. In rank order, Asia appears

can get elected by minimally democratic means, and though their policies 
and actions can lead to further economic development, they can also disrupt 
or short-circuit the modernization cycle to the point where human rights 
are violated, not respected.

as part of IMF “adjustment” policies40—can, however, be associated with

For their part, pessimists see globalization as either having the potential 
for, or a history of, delaying the growth of democracy and harming human 
rights.59 Politically, they fear that globalization will expand the hegemony of 
the West, weaken state sovereignty and lead to failed states,60 increase cross-
border corruption and smuggling,61 and result in an increase in terrorism.62 
Economically, there is concern that growth will weaken,63 unemployment 
will increase in part due to outsourcing,64 economic inequality will worsen,65 
the size and influence of labor unions will diminish,66 piracy of intellectual 
property rights will expand,67 and the imposition of financial adjustment 
plans will lead to economic stagnation and even collapse.68 
Globalization may also have a negative impact on social and cultural 
affairs. Pessimists worry about the possible breakdown of indigenous societ-
ies, increases in crime, especially drug trade and human trafficking, a growth 
in police violence, the dismemberment of the welfare state, and a rise in 
illegal migration and refugee movements.69 Finally, they are concerned that 
globalization, with its emphasis on efficiency and profits, will lead to ac-
celeration of environmental degradation.70

Exceptionalism was described by Freud71 as a condition whereby an in-
dividual feels that he or she may claim an exemption from the conventional 
constraints of society because he or she has either suffered enough or has 
been subjected to extenuating circumstances. Just as individuals can make 
the claim of exceptionalism to avoid respecting human rights,72 so too can 
countries. For example, the United States is widely seen as a country that 
makes the exceptionalist claim that its 200 years of history entitles it to its 
own foreign policies and to forge its own approach to ratifying and imple-
menting human rights.73 If the United States can make such a claim other 
countries and regions can do the same,74 and they can use the same variety 
of rationales to make their exceptionalist claim.75 The same reasoning can 
also apply to cultures—for example, Asian or Islamic values. 
Starting in the 1980s and accelerating as the Cold War came to an end, 
Asian and Islamic political leaders and public intellectuals began calling 
for an alternative to Western human rights as embodied in the Universal 
Declaration of Human rights (UDHR), which they felt was fundamentally 
flawed. Both Asian and Islamic perspectives point to the Western emphasis 
on individual rights and freedoms to the detriment of family and community 
responsibilities and values.76 They also note the emphasis on political and

twentieth and twenty-first centuries geographers have fashioned and revised 
a series of organizational and methodological “traditions.”181 In addition the 
subject matter of geography has been divided into categories: systematic 
(concentrating on specific subjects such as political geography, economic 
geography,  urban  geography,  and  environmental  geography),  regional 
geography, and spatial techniques (including quantitative and qualitative 
analysis, computer cartography, GIS, and remote sensing). Contemporary 
geographers conduct  their research within  a  multitude of  philosophical 
and epistemological orientations including positivism, humanism, Marxism 
(radical and critical), postmodernism, feminism, applied, post-structuralism, 
and post-colonialism.182 Furthermore, contemporary geographers have much 
more sophisticated means of accessing, mapping, and analyzing data than 
previously dreamed. This means that geographers focusing on human rights 
issues have multiple ways of approaching and reporting their findings. The 
challenge for human rights researchers is to discover how this variety of ap-
proaches can be incorporated in their work. For geographers the challenge 
is two-fold: to make their work more accessible to the human rights com-
munity by publishing more broadly and to incorporate the research of the 
human rights community into their own work. This dual strategy approach 
has proven useful in Asian and Jewish Studies.183
