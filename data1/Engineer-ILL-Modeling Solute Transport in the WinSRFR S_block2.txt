Modeling Solute Transport in the WinSRFR
Surface Irrigation Software

==============================

==============================
type: undefined
typeindex=3 left=134 right=396 upper=50 lower=59
====>

Eduardo Bautista, M.ASCE1; and James L. Schlegel2

type: abstract
typeindex=0 left=1 right=529 upper=91 lower=193
====>
charfont height=5.000000, charfont bold=0, alignment=0, allupper=-1, column left=-1, likeBody=100

Abstract: A solute transport model has been added to the WinSRFR surface irrigation software package. The model uses advective-
dispersive principles to predict the flow of solute with the irrigation stream and its final longitudinal distribution along the field based
on water flow predictions generated by the simulation engine. Computations are based on the fractional stepping method: advection is
computed using the method of characteristics from the velocity field computed by the simulation engine water flow calculations, and
dispersion is calculated from the advection results using a centered-difference finite-difference scheme. The Akima interpolation scheme
was used to build the method-of-characteristics solution. The computations are robust and execute in little time. The accuracy of the model
was evaluated with numerical tests and by comparison with field measurements. Results suggest that the model can generate useful
predictions under typical irrigation conditions. DOI: 10.1061/(ASCE)IR.1943-4774.0001507. © 2020 Published by American Society
of Civil Engineers.

Introduction

==============================

==============================
type: BODY
typeindex=0 left=1 right=253 upper=241 lower=528
====>
charfont height=5.000000, charfont bold=0, alignment=0, allupper=-1, column left=1, likeBody=100

A common practice in surface irrigation is the application of nitro-
gen fertilizer with the irrigation stream. In principle, fertigation pro-
motes high nutrient recovery by the crop and reduces the potential
for nutrient leaching below the root zone because it facilitates the
application closer to development stages with high nutrient de-
mand. This is difficult to do with mechanical fertilizer application
systems. However, effective fertigation practices require an irriga-
tion system with excellent distribution uniformity and application
efficiency. Moreover, even with an efficient water application, nu-
trient distribution along the length of the field and vertically within
the soil profile can be poor if the timing of the nutrient injection
relative to the flow of the irrigation stream is inadequate (Playan
and Faci 1997). In furrow irrigation systems, where infiltration is
two-dimensional, water and fertilizer distribution can be further
affected by irrigation system management variables such as flow
depth (Abbasi et al. 2003a) or the use of alternate furrow irrigation
(Ebrahimian et al. 2013).
Because of the need to understand and manage the distribution
of nutrients applied through surface irrigation systems, solute
transport modeling tools have been under development for over
20 years. This development is closely linked to the development
of tools for modeling surface irrigation flows, as well as tools
for modeling the movement of water and solutes within the soil
profile. The development discussed herein deals only with model-
ing the transport of solute with the surface and flow and its ultimate
distribution along the length of the field.

The flow of fertilizer with the irrigation stream is a special case
of advective-dispersive transport. Advection refers to the move-
ment of solutes with the mean velocity of the conveying fluid,
and thus as a result of the mean forces acting on that flow, whereas
dispersion considers the transport and mixing of the solute as a re-
sult of velocity gradients within a cross section, flow turbulence,
and molecular diffusion. Numerous studies have discussed the prin-
ciples of advective-dispersive transport in open-channel water
flows (e.g., Fischer et al. 1979; Cunge et al. 1980). The analysis
assumes a neutrally buoyant solute and relatively small concentra-
tions. These conditions imply that the solute has no effect on the
density of the water and, consequently, on the irrigation flow. The
analysis also assumes that the constituent is not subject to chemical
reactions and that the only sink is the solute lost via infiltration. If
the solute is applied in a pulse of short duration relative to the water
flow event of interest, dispersion causes the concentration of the
pulse to spread and attenuate as it travels downstream.
Solving the problem of solute advection-dispersion in surface
irrigation also requires solving the unsteady surface-flow problem
coupled to infiltration. Solution methods that have been developed
for the combined problem can be grouped in three categories. The
simplest but least accurateapproach decouples the one-dimensional
irrigation flow calculations from the solute transport, with the for-
mer executed first to provide the computational grid for the latter
(Boldt et al. 1994; Playán and Faci 1997; Strelkoff et al. 2006).
Mass balance is used to track the fate of discrete parcels of water,
with or without solute, entering a field. The parcels are defined by
the time step of the irrigation simulation. Water entering the stream
during a time step pushes water already on the field further down-
stream and does not mix with it. Water is abstracted from the parcel
byinfiltration, with the calculations conducted using the cells of the
irrigation simulation, cell by cell. This requires the water flow cells
to be calculated with an Eulerian computational grid. The remain-
ing surface water is water that can flow downstream. The parcel is
tracked in subsequent time steps until all its water is exhausted.
Because calculations allow the solute to be transported only with
the bulk flow of water, they produce a reasonable approximation to
advective transport.

solute flow calculations, but computes the latter with the partial

differential equation of advection-dispersion (ADE) (García-
Navarro et al. 2000; Abbasi et al. 2003b; Zerihun et al. 2005a;
Perea et al. 2010). This approach allows using different computa-
tional procedures for each of the two flow components and, there-
fore, allows superimposing the solute transport calculations on
an existing water flow model. As with the water flow equations,
the ADE equation can only be solved numerically for practical
problems. Solving the water and solute flow problems can fail
to properly conserve the solute, however (Murillo et al. 2005).
Furthermore, the ADE alone presents substantial computational
challenges. Because pure advection is described by a hyperbolic
partial differential equation whereas pure dispersion by a para-
bolic type equation, numerical solutions to the combined equation
can introduce numerical dispersion larger than the predicted
dispersion (Islam and Chaudhry 1997).
The fractional stepping method (also known as split operator)
was developed to mitigate this problem. In fractional stepping,
the advection component is computed first from the velocity field
computed by the water flow simulation by using the method of
characteristics to track the fate of particles introduced at the boun-
dary. Dispersion is calculated subsequently from the concentration
field computed by the advection solution. This splitting approach is
subject to a numerical error that is proportional to the calculation
time step (LeVeque 2002), Furthermore, because it requires many
interpolations, it can fail to properly conserve mass. Thus, different
variations of the method have been reported. Most decoupled fer-
tigation models have used fractional stepping to deal with the ADE
calculations (García-Navarro et al. 2000; Zerihun et al. 2005a;
Perea et al. 2010).
A third solution strategy consists of computing the water flow
and ADE equations simultaneously at every space and time step of
the calculations. Currently, the preferred approach for solving the
fully coupled problem is using finite-volume numerical procedures.
This approach has been used to model one-dimensional surface
flow cases with both borders and furrows (Burguete et al. 2009;
Zhang et al. 2016; Brunetti et al. 2018), and two-dimensional
border/basin irrigation (Dai et al. 2019). Finite-volume methods
were originally developed to model compressible flows, in which
shock waves can easily develop, and are therefore inherently better
suited for handling discontinuities in the solution and boundary
conditions than finite-difference methods. Because those methods
are computationally expensive in comparison with existing finite-
difference solutions, their application to modeling irrigation flows
is a topic of active research.
Perea et al. (2010) reported a solute transport model based on
the ADE and the fractional step solution method. This approach,
with modifications, has been added to the simulation the compo-
nent of WinSRFR version 5.1 (Bautista and Schlegel 2019), a
software package for the analysis of surface irrigations systems
developed by the USDA Agricultural Research Service. The de-
coupled solution takes advantage of the WinSRFR hydraulic sol-
ution, which runs prior to the advection-dispersion calculations.
This article documents the model and examines its strengths and
limitations.

The irrigation flow equations represent a coupled surface–
subsurface flow problem because of the infiltration term Az. SRFR
5 offers several options for representing infiltration (Bautista 2016;
Bautista and Schlegel 2019). Empirical equations assume that Az
is a function of opportunity time, i.e., the time of water contact with
the soil surface. When dealing with furrows, where infiltration is a
two-dimensional flow process, expressions are available for adjust-
ing the average intake as a function of a nominal inflow-rate-
dependent wetted perimeter. Infiltration can also be computed as
a function of soil hydraulic properties, initial soil water content,
and the variable water pressure at the soil surface, semiempirically,
with the Green-Ampt equation (borders/basins) and the Warrick-
Green-Ampt equation (furrows), or physically with the Richards
equation (only borders). Evidently, selection of an infiltration mod-
eling approach and parameterization of the selected equation play a
key role in predicting the surface flow of water, the final distribu-
tion of infiltrated water, and consequently, the infiltration of solute
along the fieldand vertically in the soil profile, but this critical issue
will not be addressed here.

Zero-Inertia/Kinematic Wave Equations

==============================

==============================
type: BODY
typeindex=0 left=277 right=529 upper=47 lower=338
====>
charfont height=5.000000, charfont bold=0, alignment=0, allupper=-1, column left=277, likeBody=100

Following Perea et al. (2010), the solution used herein decouples
the water and solute transport equations. Solution of the water flow
equations have been discussed by Walker and Skogerboe (1987),
Strelkoff (1990), Strelkoff and Clemmens (2007), and Bautista et al.
(2016). Details of those calculations will not be discussed here,
but it is relevant to this presentation to review the discretization
approach used by the SRFR simulation engine (i.e., the SRFR
dynamic-link library). SRFR solves the flow equations using an
Eulerian finite-difference scheme, with the spatial increment ini-
tially specified from the user-editable cell density parameter.
During the advance phase, the solution marches in time by advanc-
ing the stream to the next specified grid distance and, therefore,
solves for the time step. The space increments are not constant,
however, depending on spatial variations in field properties
(e.g., field elevation). Furthermore, the space step is reduced when-
ever the stream experiences substantial deceleration. In addition,
the code switches to solving for the advance distance with the time
increment known to enforce user-specified boundary conditions,
for example, changes in the inflow rate or solute injections.
During postadvance calculations, when the spatial grid is fully
defined, the solution can advance in principle using constant time
steps. However, as with the space steps, the time step is adjusted
depending on boundary conditions and computational situations,
for example, failure of the solution to converge. As a result, the
computational grid is irregular. The significance of this issue is that
numerical solutions to the ADE equation, such as the one discussed
subsequently, generally assume a uniform computational grid.

The proposed solute transport model was added to WinSRFR as a
class (SoluteTransport) of the SRFR dynamic-link library (DLL).
SoluteTransport is a subclass of a parent class that was developed
to handle different types of constituent transport calculations, to
be implemented in the future. In the SRFR DLL, the Irrigation
class handles the hydraulic simulation (Bautista et al. 2016). A
SoluteTransport object cannot be instantiated independently of an
Irrigation object, but the reverse is true. Thus, for a given data
set, users can select to run the hydraulic simulation alone or in
combination with the ADE model. In the latter case, the methods
of SoluteTransport run subsequently to those of Irrigation using
the output data objects created by Irrigation. This gives SoluteTran-
sport access to the computational grid and to flow variables for each
computational node.

Consequently, the absolute mass balance error of the modified sce-
nario is only 2%.
It should be clear that this problem can be mitigated by increas-
ing the cell density because this automatically reduces the time
step of the recession calculations. In this case, increasing the cell
density to 200 minimizes numerical dispersion and the mass-
balance error. However, the relationship between cell density and
the error is erratic, and numerical testing is needed to determine a
recommended cell density for the given conditions. In this example,
the error is minimized witha cell density of 58 and 200, but numeri-
cal dispersion effects are evident with the smaller number of cells.

implement in code but offers no discernible advantage in computa-

Discussion

==============================

==============================
type: BODY
typeindex=0 left=1 right=253 upper=335 lower=703
====>
charfont height=5.000000, charfont bold=0, alignment=0, allupper=-1, column left=1, likeBody=100

Overall, the proposed solute transport produces results that are con-
sistent with those computed with previously published models and
with field measurements. Although the Garcia advection test re-
vealed problems with the advection solution caused by the SRFR
discretization scheme, it is important to note again that the condi-
tions of that test are difficult because it involves no infiltration and
a solute pulse of very short duration. Under typical irrigation con-
ditions, slight numerical dispersion can be expected from the ad-
vection computations, but those effects can also be expected to be
eventually overshadowed by actual dispersion, even when com-
puted with the Elder equation (Ce ¼ 5.86).
The results presented in the preceding sections offer limited
guidance about the determination of the dispersivity coefficient
under irrigation conditions. Results suggest larger Kx values for
borders (Garcia and Playan tests) than for furrows (Perea test),
and perhaps larger values with increasing border width (Garcia ver-
sus Playan tests). Excellent simulation results were obtained when
applying the Fischer equation to the Perea et al. (2011) furrow data,
but that equation seem less useful for borders because Kx varies
with the square of the top flow width. An example is the Playan
test, for which the Kx computed with the Fischer equation was
1–3 orders of magnitude greater than values computed with other
equations, even though the test border was only 3.84 m wide.
Much larger Kx estimates can be expected for typical border dimen-
sions of perhaps 50–100 m. The Rutherford equation can likewise
produce very large estimates with large top widths.
Additional fertigation data sets were sought to test Kx but the
available published data sets (Zerihun et al. 2005b; Brunetti et al.
2018) proved of limited value for that purpose. Those tests applied
the solute pulse over at least half of the irrigation duration into
blocked irrigation systems. Under those conditions, the flow veloc-
ity eventually becomes zero or near-zero, advective-dispersive
transport becomes insignificant, and molecular diffusion becomes
the primary solute transport mechanism.

==============================

==============================
type: BODY
typeindex=0 left=277 right=529 upper=1 lower=193
====>
charfont height=5.000000, charfont bold=0, alignment=0, allupper=-1, column left=277, likeBody=100

Reported fertigation models have proven capable of reproduc-
ing measured solute hydrographs with reasonable accuracy under
the range of conditions tested by their authors. This model is no
exception. This relative accuracy is explained in part by the fact
that the fertigation problem is relatively simple in comparison with
similar problems such as solute transport in rivers. Irrigation flows
are always subcritical and the channel is short and prismatic or
nearly prismatic. The solute pulse is well-defined and can be ex-
pected to fully mix with the stream by the time the water enters the
field. Advection predictions are highly dependent on the calibration
of the water flow parameters (infiltration and roughness). Conse-
quently, the travel time of a nonreactive solute plume is well pre-
dicted if the water flow is well-predicted. Lastly, because the solute
injection typically occurs over a long time relative to the duration of
the irrigation event, numerical errors induced by discontinuities in
the boundary conditions (start and stop of an irrigation pulse) can
be expected to, ultimately, have a limited impact on the accuracy of
the solution.

Conclusions

==============================

==============================
type: BODY
typeindex=0 left=277 right=529 upper=243 lower=380
====>
charfont height=5.000000, charfont bold=0, alignment=0, allupper=-1, column left=277, likeBody=100

A nonreactive solute transport model based on the advection-
dispersion equation was added to WinSRFR, a software package
for the hydraulic analysis of surface irrigation systems. Simulations
were reasonably accurate under the range of irrigation conditions
tested, but results also showed that some inaccuracies could emerge
as a result of the discretization scheme used by the hydraulic sim-
ulation model, which underlies the ADE solution. Results also re-
vealed limitations of procedures currently available for determining
the coefficient of dispersivity. Although solute transport in typical
irrigation flows can be expected to be advection-dominated, a better
understanding of the range of plausible values for the dispersivity
coefficient is needed. Studies need to be conducted under various
field conditions to establish this range.
