federal  legislation  aimed  at  providing  services  to  students

points  out  that  the  LD field  suffers  from  an  identity  crisis,

precedence  over  a  natural  science  perspective;  instructional 
programs  were  implemented  and  services delivered,  but  the 
formal study of LD took on a secondary and lesser importance.

Despite warnings that the field of learning disabilities 
(LD) must address the issue of population heterogeneity, the LD 
research community still lacks operational definitions of specific 
learning disabilities as well as a systematic approach for reporting 
sample characteristics. Recently, however, a definition of dyslexia 
has been proposed that might signal a significant advance. This 
article builds on this definition of dyslexia by: (a) reintroducing 
the concept of marker variables, (b) proposing a strategy for 
developing an updated marker variable system, (c) presenting 
a preliminary working set of dyslexia marker variables, and 
(d) calling for the eventual adoption of this or similar marker 
variable systems to facilitate future research efforts.

That is not to say that, faced with this situation, profes­
sionals failed to issue words of caution. Soon after LD became 
federally  recognized  disability  category, 
Reid,  Gandara,  and  Omori  (1978)  warned  against  ignoring 
the  natural  science  investigation  of  LD.  Foremost  among 
their concerns was that many researchers used the term “learn­
ing disabilities” in  a generic sense,  failing to recognize that 
individuals  with  LD  comprised  a  heterogeneous  population 
experiencing a multitude of learning difficulties: “Every cur­
sory review of the literature suggests that many of the prob­
lems of definition of learning disabilities, and thus of selection 
and intervention, are directly attributable to the heterogeneity 
of individuals categorized as learning disabled” (Keogh et al., 
1978, p. 18).
To address this concern, Keogh, Major-Kingsley, Omori- 
Gordon, and Reid (1982) proposed a system of marker vari­
ables to obtain a clearer  picture  of the  individual with LD. 
Without  the  collection  of  participant-specific  information, 
Keogh et al. warned, researchers would be at risk of defining 
their  samples  in  different  ways,  gathering  inconsistent  data, 
and conducting studies that would be impossible to replicate. 
The  marker  variables  proposed  by  Keogh  et  al.  would  be 
“agreed-upon reference points or ‘bench marks’ on which to 
base comparisons and generalizations of results across stud­
ies or programs, and across ages, backgrounds, or geographic 
locations of samples” (Keogh et al., 1982, p. 7). Once estab­
lished, these markers would promote consistency in research 
terminology,  measurement  techniques,  and  reporting  proce­
dures.
In spite of  this early warning, a clear conceptualization 
of LD and the development of an agreed-upon set of marker 
variables  were  not  achieved.  As  a  result,  methodological 
flaws, such as the lack of clearly defined samples of individ­
uals identified as having  LD (e.g., Durrant, 1994; Hammill, 
Bryant, Brown, Dunn, & Marten, 1989), abound in the empirical 
investigation of LD in spite of two attempts by the Research 
Committee of the Council for Learning Disabilities (Rosen­
berg et al., 1992; Smith etai., 1984) to set minimum standards 
for reporting participant information.
These findings confirmed Keogh et al.’s (1978) predic­
tion that without a clear conceptualization of LD and a marker 
variable system  much  of  the  research  conducted  during  the 
1980s  is  “largely  nonreplicable  and  therefore  presumably 
invalid”  (Hammill  et  al.,  1989,  p.  178;  see Note).  Durrant 
(1994) indicated that the issue that Keogh et al. (1982) warned 
about—defining and describing heterogeneity in the LD popu­
lation—is currently “almost completely ignored, despite dem­
onstrations of its importance” (Durrant, 1994, p. 31 ). In short, 
the  population  of  youngsters  identified  in  the  literature  as 
having LD continues to be treated as a homogeneous popula­
tion, and researchers fail to be consistent in their participant 
sampling and reporting procedures. A primary reason for this 
dilemma is the lack of a clear operational definition of learn­
ing disabilities; that is,  a definition grounded  in theory and 
considered  to  have  construct  validity  based  on  empirical 
research and clinical observations.

Echoing the remarks of Keogh et al. (1982), Stanovich (cited 
in Lyon, 1995) suggested that the LD field’s attempt to define 
generic LD comprehensively was a large part of the problem: 
“scientific  investigations  of  some  generically  defined  entity 
called  ‘learning  disability’  simply  make  little  sense  given 
what  we  already  know  about  heterogeneity  across  various 
learning  domains”  (p. 5).  Instead  of  examining LD  in  this 
fashion and ignoring that heterogeneity exists, future research 
should “define groups specifically in terms of the domain of 
deficit  (reading  disability,  arithmetic  disability)”  (Stanovich, 
cited  in Lyon, 1995, p. 5). In other words, different defini­
tions should be formulated for a variety of learning disabili­
ties, each one pertaining to a specific deficit.
Although  previous  attempts  had  been  made  to  provide 
more  refined  global  or  generic  definitions  of  LD  (National 
Joint Committee on Learning Disabilities, 1994; see also Ham­

Society Research Committee (ODSRC) was among  the first 
organizations  in  the  LD  field  to propose  a  domain-specific 
definition of one type of learning disability—dyslexia:

Liberman,  Shankweiler,  &  Liberman,  1989;  Perfetti,  1985; 
Share  &  Stanovich, 1995)  that  supported the  statement that 
“[Dyslexia] is a specific  language-based disorder .. . usually 
reflecting insufficient phonological processing abilities’’ (Lyon, 
1995, p. 9). Lyon provided similar theoretical and empirical 
evidence for each of the five categories, thereby satisfying the 
first two criteria—that  definitions must be theory driven and 
supported  by convergent research  and clinical information— 
by  which  he  asserted  that all  domain-specific  definitions  of 
learning disabilities should be judged.
Closer inspection of the five criteria  proposed by Lyon 
(1995) shows  that not two but three of his proposed criteria 
were satisfied: (a) the ODSRC definition was certainly theory 
driven;  (b)  each  component  was  adequately  supported  by 
existing empirical research; and, given his thorough review of 
the literature, (c) the definition was definitely based on theo­
retical  conceptualizations  of  dyslexia.  However,  two  of  the 
five criteria cited by Lyon have not yet been satisfied; in par­
ticular, without a system of marker variables to address sample 
variability, a definition of dyslexia cannot be based on well- 
defined subject samples. Without such a marker variable sys­
tem, and without clearly defined subject samples, it would be 
impossible to ascertain whether or not the proposed definition 
is externally valid and useful. In other words, a truly opera­
tional  definition of dyslexia  cannot  be  formulated without a 
set of established criteria or marker variables.
To satisfy this definitional criterion, a strategy for devel­
oping  a  set  of  dyslexia  marker  variables  is  proposed  here. 
This marker variable  system  must be derived from domain­
specific definitions of LD. Generic conceptualizations of LD 
have proven more of a hindrance than a help to guide research 
efforts. The choice of dyslexia as the prototype for domain­
specific  definitions  is  a  logical  one,  given  the  multitude  of 
studies already  conducted nationwide.  Moreover, this marker 
variable system must be developed concurrently with its asso­
ciated domain-specific definition. Furthermore, both the domain­
specific definition and the marker variable system must conform 
to an accepted set of criteria, such as those proposed by Lyon 
(1995): They must be theory driven, supported by convergent 
research,  grounded  on  internally  valid  theoretical  constructs 
subject to objective measurement, based on well-defined sub­
ject samples, and ultimately proven to be externally valid. As 
the ODSRC has already proposed a definition of dyslexia, the 
first step has been taken. The next step involves the develop­
ment of a working dyslexia marker variable system to better 
define subject samples.

variable would have to be defined operationally, and researchers 
were  urged  to  provide  a  description  of  specific  techniques 
used to measure the variable of interest.

coordination, (f)  memory, (g)  oral  language,  and (h)  visual 
perception.
